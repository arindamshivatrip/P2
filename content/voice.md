Q: What are you working on right now?
A: Honestly, my thesis, for one. It builds on Reframed, which I've already exhibited a few times, and I'm working with Dr. Beth Bonsignore and Dr. Ming Lin at UMD. Other than that, my e-ink ambient reader, which started as a personal project and is turning into an AI-enabled planner and life assistant. And I'm heading to Android Dev Summit.

Q: Why XR?
A: That's a fascinating question. I think XR is a new frontier for how we live in the embodied internet age. AR lets you interact with the world more naturally without being distracted from it, and VR lets you experience worlds that would otherwise be incomprehensible. Immersion at one end, no distraction at the other. That's truly revolutionary to me.

Q: Why did you go from computer engineering to HCI?
A: I didn't go into undergrad wanting to do HCI. I stumbled on it during my interactive media concentration at NUS and realized computing needed to be recentered on people. Code that's built around what the user actually needs is what provides real value. And I'm a people person, so HCI lets me play to both strengths: the technical side and working with people.

Q: Tell me about the helmet scanning project.
A: TL;DR: LIGHT Helmets are modular, with pad zones where you stack pads to different heights. Every head is shaped differently, so one-size-fits-all doesn't really work, and fitting was all done manually. They wanted an end-to-end pipeline from scanning to a final pad map the fitter can just follow. The catch was data. With clean data this could've been a machine learning problem, but it wasn't, so I worked in 3D geometry and positioning instead. I got it to a pretty standardized sizing algorithm that does the job well, though pad modeling could still use more sophisticated algorithms.

Q: What's Aura Desk?
A: Aura Desk came out of the Android XR hackathon at AWE. I'd seen a YouTube video where someone put a projector over their desk and turned it into an ambient workspace with pinned widgets, and I thought, what if you did that with AR? Especially on Project Aura, since it's optical pass-through. It also has on-device AI, so you can launch music or a browser by voice. Kind of a real-life Jarvis.

Q: Why should we hire you?
A: Because I'm determined. Once I start on a problem, I can't stop myself until I finish it. I know design, I can code and rapid-prototype, and I know data viz. I've dabbled in a bit of everything, so I fit in anywhere. People quote "jack of all trades, master of none," but the second half is "oftentimes better than a master of one." For creative technologist roles, that's exactly what you need.

Q: What's your biggest weakness?
A: Sometimes I care about a project a little too much. When I'm working with people, that makes it hard to delegate the team's work, because it's difficult to trust that everyone will work the way I do or hold themselves to the same standard.

Q: Are you more of a designer or an engineer?
A: Honestly, a bit of both, but more of an engineer. I'm an engineer who can design rather than a designer who can code. That's just what I've always been into, plus my undergrad is in computer engineering.

Q: What kind of role are you looking for?
A: XR engineering, creative technologist, prototyping, UX engineering, or human-centered AI. I'm looking for co-ops starting in December and full-time roles after May 2027. Open to relocating anywhere in the US, or remote.

Q: What's the hardest bug you've dealt with?
A: The night before my undergrad capstone was due. That was ShooT IT, an AR laser tag system. We were down to four people instead of five, and I owned the mobile AR. The AR side just wouldn't connect to the rest of the system and the scoring logic, and this was pre-ChatGPT. It turned out to be a weird server config error on my end, but if I hadn't found it, the whole project would've fallen apart, and it would've been squarely my fault.
